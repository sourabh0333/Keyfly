# Show the edit rivals keep hidden

Competing AI video editors present their products in one of two ways. Some build SEO catalogues of hundreds of near-identical tool pages; the rest build a few flagship pages that all follow one template: hero, logo wall, four to six feature blocks, a four-step "how it works", testimonials, FAQ, and the same "Get started free" button repeated down the page. Cardboard, the main competitor, has **no product or features page at all**. Its product story is spread across the homepage, a Mac page, a plugins page, a recipe library and a weekly changelog, and its strongest capabilities (a director that checks its own work, reference matching, web-sourced B-roll) appear mainly in the changelog and pricing table. Across all sixteen vendors, proof is mostly borrowed scale (logo walls, user counts, G2 badges), and it often contradicts itself. Demos are mostly screenshots, AI decisions show up as one opaque score, and timeline hand-off, platform details, data handling and pricing units are either undersold or confused. KeyFly cannot compete on logos or metrics and must not invent them. Its Product page should therefore win on what rivals hide: one project traced from a sentence to a real timeline, the AI director's report explained field by field, a spec sheet a buyer can check (formats, editors, platforms, agents), and a plain account of ownership, data and AI minutes. The recommended page has six sections grouped by stage under a sticky sub-navigation (Make, Direct, Footage, Brand, Deliver, Your way). It has one primary call to action at the top and one at the close, contextual links in between, and real screen recordings of the app's interface as the main asset to request from the team. About twenty claims need the team's sign-off before launch.

## Rivals build catalogues; only a few pages show one flow

The URL estates show two strategies. VEED's tools hub lists "several hundred" single-tool pages in a flat A–Z directory with no category headings ([VEED Tools](https://www.veed.io/tools)). Kapwing lists roughly 400 A–Z entries plus a "Just For Fun" block ([Kapwing Tools](https://www.kapwing.com/tools)). Descript runs a /features hub, about 19 core feature pages, 18 /ai/* pages and **300+ /tools/* long-tail pages** ([Descript sitemap](https://www.descript.com/lp/sitemap-pages.xml)). Submagic adds 33 "Submagic vs X" logo cards with no criteria ([Submagic /vs](https://www.submagic.co/vs)). These pages exist to capture search traffic, not to explain a product. The opposite strategy is a short list of opinionated flagship pages: Riverside's /agents and /ai ([Riverside agents](https://riverside.com/agents)), OpusClip's /clipanything and /mcp ([OpusClip MCP](https://www.opus.pro/mcp)), HeyGen's /agent ([HeyGen /agent](https://www.heygen.com/agent)), and Adobe's curated Premiere hub of **7 tabs and 41 cards**, about six per stage, each linking to a deep page ([Premiere features](https://www.adobe.com/products/premiere/features.html)). The clearest SaaS model outside video is Linear's /features: **8 capabilities in two tiers** (2 primary, 6 secondary), each card linking to its own sub-page ([Linear features](https://linear.app/features)).

Cardboard is the outlier. Its sitemap has **137 URLs but only 13 core pages**, and none is /product, /features, /use-cases, /compare or /integrations ([cardboard.ai sitemap](https://cardboard.ai/sitemap.xml)). The homepage carries the product story. Its three-step "Describe it / Direct it / Share it" runs into three feature groups ("Build the story", "Shape the edit", "Work and publish your way"), then a Mac promo, use-case categories, pricing and an FAQ ([cardboard.ai](https://cardboard.ai/)). Depth lives on satellites. /desktop runs a 1:05 demo, "Faster editing, straight from your Mac" and a three-step agent connection ([cardboard.ai/desktop](https://www.cardboard.ai/desktop)). /mcp shows a mock ChatGPT window and recipe cards that pre-fill a prompt with "Try in ChatGPT" ([cardboard.ai/mcp](https://www.cardboard.ai/mcp)). /recipes holds **about 86 recipes** with "Remix" buttons ([sitemap](https://cardboard.ai/sitemap.xml)). A dedicated KeyFly Product page is therefore a structural difference from Cardboard, not an imitation of it.

Where vendors do group features, they group them by workflow stage rather than by technology. Descript's /features runs five numbered stages (Record, Edit, Refine, Share, Multiply), about 30 cards and a 22-term glossary ([descript.com/features](https://www.descript.com/features)). Synthesia's platform page runs Create, Translate, Manage, Publish and Engage, with **about 24 features and a customer quote in almost every section** ([Synthesia /video](https://www.synthesia.io/video)). Pictory's menus read Create, Edit and Scale ([Pictory home](https://pictory.ai/)). The long pages run 10 to 19 sections, and the feature-heavy ones push "how it works" into the middle. The tightest is HeyGen's /agent: five promise-phrased sections ("See Your Video Before It's Made", "Edit Everything After Rendering") of three bullets each ([HeyGen /agent](https://www.heygen.com/agent)). Every vendor uses a three- or four-step explainer, and the steps are interchangeable. **No vendor shows one prompt visibly becoming a script, scenes, a voice-over and a timeline in a single synchronised view.** They rely on numbered lists, screenshots or a poster image instead ([InVideo AI video generator](https://invideo.io/make/ai-video-generator/); [Pictory idea to video](https://pictory.ai/idea-to-video)).

## Proof is mostly borrowed scale, and much of it contradicts itself

The category's default proof is a logo wall plus a large number. Descript shows a 15-logo wall twice and **G2 4.6 from 837 reviews**, and reuses the same two customer metrics (SaaStr "100%" more content, LinkedIn "10+ clips") across pages ([descript.com/video-editing](https://www.descript.com/video-editing); [descript.com/mcp](https://www.descript.com/mcp)). HeyGen's /agent shows exact counters such as "178,728,948 videos generated" ([HeyGen /agent](https://www.heygen.com/agent)). Runway claims "60M+ users" ([Runway /product](https://runway.com/product)). The numbers frequently disagree with themselves. Synthesia's generator page says "1M+ users" in one section and "2M+" in its CTA ([Synthesia AI video generator](https://www.synthesia.io/features/ai-video-generator)). HeyGen's /alternatives gives G2 4.8 and 4.7, "500+" and "1000+" avatars, and "500,000+ users" against "85,000 customers" ([HeyGen /alternatives](https://www.heygen.com/alternatives)). Kapwing's AI page says both 9 and 18 integrated models ([Kapwing AI](https://www.kapwing.com/ai/overview)). OpusClip gives caption accuracy as "over 99%" in the page text and 97% in its structured data ([OpusClip home](https://www.opus.pro/)). Submagic gives its G2 score as 4.9 on the homepage and 4.7 on a feature page ([Magic Clips](https://www.submagic.co/features/magic-clips)). InVideo's enterprise page claims "10X" speed and "$12K" saved per piece of content with no source ([InVideo enterprise](https://invideo.io/enterprise/)). Cardboard's "Create stunning content 10x faster" is unsourced too ([cardboard.ai/desktop](https://www.cardboard.ai/desktop)).

Cardboard is the one competitor that, like KeyFly, has no customer logos or testimonials. It proves itself with artefacts instead: a demo video, interface mockups, the recipe gallery and a **weekly, numbered changelog with a video per entry** ([cardboard.ai/changelog](https://www.cardboard.ai/changelog)). Its blog comparisons are more credible than Descript's tables because they admit weaknesses: no free trial, no Teams plan, no real-time co-editing, and an Apple-silicon-only Mac app with no Windows build ([Premiere Pro vs Cardboard](https://www.cardboard.ai/blog/premiere-pro-cardboard-ai-compared)). Descript's own comparison against CapCut scores **13 wins, 0 losses and 5 ties** ([Descript vs CapCut](https://www.descript.com/compare/descript-vs-capcut)). The lesson for KeyFly: specific, checkable and honest beats big and round.

Interactive proof is rare and works well. Captions' homepage has a working upload widget over 20 style thumbnails and a "same video, three styles" demo ([Captions home](https://www.captions.ai/)). Synthesia's and HeyGen's generator pages put a live prompt box in the hero ([Synthesia AI video generator](https://www.synthesia.io/features/ai-video-generator); [HeyGen /agent](https://www.heygen.com/agent)). The browser-first editors (VEED, Kapwing, CapCut) offer nothing to try on the page, only screenshots, posters and one YouTube walkthrough ([VEED AI Video Agent](https://www.veed.io/tools/video-gpt/ai-video-agent)). Navattic's benchmark of 28,000+ demos supports trying over watching. Top-1% interactive demos reach **84.4% engagement and 54.0% CTA click-through**; the best have **5–12 steps** with one CTA each; about **71% of top demos are ungated**, and ungated demos engage 10% more. The data is desktop-heavy: **88% of sessions are on desktop**, and desktop click-through runs 52% higher than mobile, so a mobile fallback matters ([Navattic 2025](https://www.navattic.com/report/state-of-the-interactive-product-demo-2025)). This is vendor data, biased toward companies that bought a demo tool, but it points the same way as the competitor evidence. KeyFly's homepage already has a working prompt box and scroll-played editor demos, which puts it ahead of most of this field. The Product page should keep that lead without repeating the homepage's components.

The legal backdrop rewards restraint. In Operation AI Comply (September 2024) the FTC took five actions against exaggerated or fake AI claims, including a **$193,000 settlement with DoNotPay** and a case against Rytr for AI-generated fake reviews ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2024/10/FTC-Targets-Unfair-or-Deceptive-AI-Practices-With-Five-New-Enforcement-Actions)). FTC guidance asks advertisers whether they exaggerate what their AI does and whether "better than non-AI" claims are substantiated ([Kelley Drye on FTC guidance](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/ftc-reminds-advertisers-to-keep-ai-claims-in-check)). Google's SoftwareApplication rich results require a real `aggregateRating` or `review`, and violations can bring manual action ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/software-app)). KeyFly has neither, so it should not add that markup.

## What good product pages do in 2026

The evidence supports a small set of practices. **The Product page is a hub for evaluators, not a second homepage.** Visitors arrive mid-funnel and want mechanism and proof. NN/g's 293-site B2B study separates "users" from "choosers" (the people who approve the purchase) and says pages must serve both ([NN/g B2B report](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/b2b-websites-usability/)). For KeyFly, the editor cares about control, export formats and agents; the payer cares about AI-minute cost, data and ownership. **Group by the user's job, cap the groups, and tier them.** Linear's 2 + 6 and Adobe's AI page are the models; the Adobe page leads with three headline AI capabilities, then an "even more" tier of eight and a "more" tier of five ([Premiere AI video editing](https://www.adobe.com/products/premiere/ai-video-editing.html)). Observed practice is 6–10 top-level capabilities. No controlled study fixes the number. **Use in-page navigation once the page passes a few screens**: Descript's tools page puts eight anchor tabs in its hero ([Descript tools](https://descript.com/tools)).

On calls to action, discipline beats volume. VEED's agent page repeats one verb-first CTA ("Enter the AI playground") at three fixed points ([VEED AI Video Agent](https://www.veed.io/tools/video-gpt/ai-video-agent)). CapCut's suite page repeats "Try for free" about **28 times** ([CapCut creative suite](https://www.capcut.com/creative-suite)), and HeyGen's /agent uses "Get Started For Free" seven times ([HeyGen /agent](https://www.heygen.com/agent)). Outcome-phrased CTAs read better than generic ones: InVideo's "Upload footage to your first project→" and Pictory's "Turn your first idea into a video" ([InVideo home](https://invideo.io/); [Pictory idea to video](https://pictory.ai/idea-to-video)). Free terms must be consistent. VEED says "No credit card required" while its FAQ says the playground needs a subscription ([VEED AI Video Agent](https://www.veed.io/tools/video-gpt/ai-video-agent)). Cardboard shows Lite as free on the Mac and $5 on the web, and its changelog mentions a three-day trial its pricing page doesn't offer ([cardboard.ai/pricing](https://www.cardboard.ai/pricing); [changelog](https://www.cardboard.ai/changelog)).

AI-specific trust is about calibration, not maximisation. Google's People + AI Guidebook tells products to say plainly what the AI can and cannot do so users "calibrate their trust" ([PAIR](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/explainability-trust/)). Category leaders now surface model choice and safety on the product page: Runway names its models and links to a Safety page ([Runway /product](https://runway.com/product)), and Linear gives AI and Security their own pages ([Linear features](https://linear.app/features)). Riverside's framings, "AI that plays by your rules" and "choose the level of handoff", are the closest analogue in the category to KeyFly's "your way" positioning ([Riverside AI](https://riverside.com/ai); [Riverside agents](https://riverside.com/agents)). For comparisons, the FTC permits naming competitors if the basis is clear and truthful ([FTC policy statement](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statement-policy-regarding-comparative-advertising)). KeyFly's rule against naming competitors points instead to Pictory's lower-risk format: an "AI vs the traditional way" table ([Pictory AI video editor](https://pictory.ai/ai-video-editor)).

Performance and accessibility constrain how much motion the page can carry. **WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.2 (Level A)** requires a pause, stop or hide control on any content that moves automatically for more than five seconds ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). That applies to every self-running demo; KeyFly's homepage demos should be checked against it too. Lazy-loading media in the first viewport hurts LCP: CrUX median p75 LCP was **2,922 ms without lazy loading and 3,546 ms with it** (2020–21 data, correlational) ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/lcp-lazy-loading)). The hero media should load eagerly with a poster, and everything below it should load on view and pause offscreen, as KeyFly's `PauseOffscreen` pattern already does.

## Five gaps the competitors leave open

**First, nobody shows the transformation.** Every vendor uses a three- or four-step list. None shows one prompt visibly producing its storyboard, voice-over, motion graphics and timeline layers in sync ([Pictory idea to video](https://pictory.ai/idea-to-video); [HeyGen /agent](https://www.heygen.com/agent)). Edit-by-words examples are thin: three commands at InVideo, one chat swap at Pictory ([InVideo AI video generator](https://invideo.io/make/ai-video-generator/); [Pictory AI video editor](https://pictory.ai/ai-video-editor)).

**Second, AI decisions are a black box with one number on it.** OpusClip's virality score is a 0–99 ranking built from Hook, Flow, Value and Trend, with no written reasons, and free users can't see it ([OpusClip help](https://help.opus.pro/docs/article/virality-score)). Captions and Submagic rank clips the same way without explaining them ([Create clips](https://www.captions.ai/features/create-clips)). Riverside's "nothing publishes without approval" is the strongest control statement in the field, and it does not mention undo ([Riverside agents](https://riverside.com/agents)). Cardboard is closer than anyone. Its Director "shows its plan" and allows undo, and its changelog announced "The Agent Got Eyes" and "Director checks its own work". Those claims sit in the changelog and on /desktop, not on a page built around them ([cardboard.ai/desktop](https://www.cardboard.ai/desktop); [changelog](https://www.cardboard.ai/changelog)). KeyFly's director reports its hook, voice (with alternatives that "also fit"), music, assumptions, a self-check score and "Next" ideas, with Revert and Retry. That is the most complete decision display in the category. The honest framing is that KeyFly shows this most completely, not that it alone has it.

**Third, timeline hand-off is undersold or missing.** The browser-first editors (VEED, Kapwing, CapCut, Canva) advertise no FCPXML, EDL or OTIO export on the pages read ([Kapwing AI](https://www.kapwing.com/ai/overview); [CapCut Desktop](https://www.capcut.com/tools/desktop-video-editor)). Others offer it but bury it. Descript lists EDL, AAF, FCPXML and Premiere/Resolve XML, but only on its MCP page ([descript.com/mcp](https://www.descript.com/mcp)). Cardboard puts Premiere, Final Cut and Resolve export inside its paid-plan list ([cardboard.ai](https://cardboard.ai/)). OpusClip's XML export is reportedly Pro-only ([OpusClip pricing](https://opus.pro/pricing), via search summary). KeyFly exports FCPXML, EDL CMX 3600 (which also reaches Avid) and OpenTimelineIO, plus "Package for editing" (a .zip of the timeline with all its media). That is broader and more open than any competitor's marketing shows, and it deserves its own block with a format-by-editor table.

**Fourth, "bring your own agent" is now table stakes, so the edge is in the specifics.** Cardboard connects Claude and Codex through a paid Claude or ChatGPT plan ([cardboard.ai/desktop](https://www.cardboard.ai/desktop)). InVideo names Claude Cowork, Codex, Grok and Gemini ([InVideo home](https://invideo.io/)). Descript's MCP covers Claude, ChatGPT and Cursor ([descript.com/mcp](https://www.descript.com/mcp)). Kapwing's CTA reads "Try with Claude or ChatGPT" ([Kapwing AI Video Editor](https://www.kapwing.com/ai/copilot)). OpusClip's MCP draws on the same credit balance, which agents can query ([OpusClip MCP](https://www.opus.pro/mcp)). KeyFly's distinctions are concrete: Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Antigravity or any ACP agent, connected through the desktop app, **using no AI minutes**, alongside KeyFly Cloud, which picks a model per step. Platforms give a citable difference as well. Cardboard's Mac app is Apple-silicon only and its Windows version is "coming" ([cardboard.ai/desktop](https://www.cardboard.ai/desktop)). Captions and Runway are mobile and web only ([Captions home](https://www.captions.ai/); [Runway home](https://runway.com/)). KeyFly runs on Apple-silicon and Intel Macs, Windows 10 and 11, the web, and inside ChatGPT and Claude.

**Fifth, cost and data are explained badly.** Cardboard sells two credit types ("usage" and "generative"), alongside a list of model names ([cardboard.ai/pricing](https://www.cardboard.ai/pricing)). Descript's tier prices appear as two unlabelled numbers ([descript.com/underlord](https://www.descript.com/underlord)). OpusClip's page text says credits while its structured data says minutes ([OpusClip home](https://www.opus.pro/)). On data, Cardboard's "footage stays on your Mac" is qualified only in its FAQ, which says frames and transcript are sent for edits ([cardboard.ai/desktop](https://www.cardboard.ai/desktop)). InVideo's enterprise page has raised buyer expectations with "no training on customer data" and commercial indemnification ([InVideo enterprise](https://invideo.io/enterprise/)). KeyFly can state one unit (AI minutes, counted only while the AI works), say precisely when desktop footage leaves the machine (only when a cloud AI step needs it), and use the app's own line, "Your projects stay yours". It should make no training or retention claims until the privacy policy is final.

A smaller gap concerns the brand kit. Most vendors list it as storage ("set once, applied automatically"). Pictory and VEED fill a kit from a website URL, so that ability is not unique ([Pictory AI video editor](https://pictory.ai/ai-video-editor); [VEED](https://www.veed.io/learn/branded-videos-at-scale)). What KeyFly adds is tone of voice and a voice-over (AI or cloned) inside the kit, applied to every new video. The way to show it is the fill itself: a URL in, a kit out.

## Conclusion

The research changes the brief. The question is not how KeyFly's Product page can list more features than its competitors; it is how the page can explain one product completely enough that a buyer never needs a logo wall. The competitors' biggest weakness is a credibility deficit: contradictory numbers, one-sided tables, opaque scores and free terms that disagree with their own FAQs. A page built only from facts the app itself shows, with illustrations labelled as such, can be checked line by line and will read as more trustworthy. Cardboard is the one rival following a similar artefact-led strategy, and its weakness is scatter: its best ideas sit in a changelog. KeyFly wins by gathering the equivalent material into one organised page and by showing two things Cardboard can't: the hand-off to every major editor and the choice of platform and AI.

Two consequences follow. The most valuable asset for this page is not new illustration but **real screen recordings of the app's interface**: the director's report, the export sheet, Settings → Agents, the brand kit fill. The app's output isn't ready for a "made with" gallery, but its interface already shows these things, and interface footage is honest proof that doesn't depend on output quality. Second, a public changelog is the cheapest credible proof KeyFly could add, and Cardboard shows it works, but only if the team commits to a regular cadence. The section-by-section plan follows.

## Recommended KeyFly Product page plan

**URL and role.** Use `/product`, reached from the header's "Product" item. (The header currently points Product at the homepage's `#product`; repoint it once the page exists.) The page is the organised reference behind the homepage's story. The homepage dramatises each idea once; the Product page explains how each one works, what it produces and what it costs, and links out to sub-pages as they are built. It should reuse primitives (`Section`, `SectionHeading`, `Button`, `Preview`, `Shot`, `Divider`) but **not** re-mount homepage showpieces (`Anatomy`, `Story`, `CommandCut`, `Inspector`, `Morph`, `BrandReel`, `Doors`, `Handoff`). Where a homepage idea recurs, the Product page shows its mechanism or spec, not its demo. Keep one self-running element on screen at a time, give each a visible pause control (WCAG 2.2.2), and label every visual "Illustrative" until real recordings replace it. The page stays `noindex` with the rest of the site until the copy is confirmed.

### Section notes beyond the table

**Hero.** The H1 must say "AI video editor" in plain words, as the single H1 for search and scanning, with the accent in the serif italic per the design system. The project map does the job Linear's capability cards do (each node is a deep link) while staying visual. On phones it becomes a sideways-scrolling row of nodes. The fact row uses counts that are simply true, so it can never contradict itself the way Synthesia's and HeyGen's counters do. It must hold no user numbers, speed claims or ratings. The hero media loads eagerly with a poster; nothing in the first viewport is lazy-loaded.

**Make.** This section goes deeper than the homepage's illustrative film. Instead of one finished cut, it pulls one prompt apart into its layers and labels each with what made it and which plan includes it. Tier labels must come from the pricing API, not be typed into the page. Generated video and voice cloning carry a "higher plans" tag drawn from the API's plan data. It is the place for the music, sound-effects and AI-image assets, which the homepage barely mentions.

**Direct.** This is the page's centrepiece and its clearest win over the field, where the best on offer is one opaque 0–99 score. The homepage's "You make the calls" plays the idea as a demo; here it becomes a reference card: each field of the report, what it means, and what you can do about it. Pair it with an honest "What the AI does / What you decide" block (the PAIR guidance): the AI writes and assembles, and you approve, change, revert or retry. Never imply the AI is always right. If the self-check score is shown, explain its scale in one line or leave the number out. A real screen recording of the report panel would be the page's strongest single asset.

**Footage.** The homepage's "Your footage" contrasts chores with one sentence. Here, each cleanup gets its own small before/after with a one-line explanation, and a precise data note follows. This is the answer to Cardboard's "footage stays on your Mac" problem: say exactly when footage leaves the machine and why, in one sentence, on the page, not in an FAQ.

**Brand.** Show the fill as a transformation, because static brand-kit bullets are the category norm. Voice-over and tone of voice are the fields competitors don't show. Mention the Team plan's shared brand kits only with wording that matches the API.

**Deliver.** The format-by-editor table is the page's most "checkable" block. It is the professional hand-off competitors bury on MCP pages or in plan lists. Name the editors in text only (no third-party logos, as on the homepage's Tools section). Share-for-review belongs here; note that Cardboard also offers timestamped comments ([changelog](https://www.cardboard.ai/changelog)), so present it as a capability, not a differentiator.

**Your way.** This section carries the positioning ("The AI video editor, your way") as a ledger of what KeyFly doesn't lock you into. It is where Windows and Intel Mac support, own agents with no AI minutes, and "projects never expire" sit side by side. Enterprise items (SSO/SCIM, DPA, data residency) appear as plain facts with a contact route, not as compliance badges KeyFly hasn't earned (no SOC 2 or ISO claims).

**AI minutes.** It is normal in this category to put pricing on the product page, but the homepage already renders the API-fed table. A single explanatory block that links to it avoids duplication and fixes the confusion the field creates (two credit types; credits and minutes used interchangeably).

**What to leave out.** No "made with KeyFly" gallery (the output isn't ready). No logos, testimonials, user counts, ratings or speed and time-saving figures. No named-competitor comparison. No repeat of the homepage's 14-question FAQ. If the team wants an FAQ, use four to six technical questions answered from the app (file formats, project expiry, agents and AI minutes, platforms) that are not already on the homepage.

### Sub-navigation

A sticky in-page bar appears once the hero scrolls away and sits under the header's floating pill. It holds six anchors: **Make · Direct · Footage · Brand · Deliver · Your way**, plus a quiet "Pricing" link to `#minutes` at its end. The current section is marked with the mint underline used by the header's sliding light. When the header slides away on scroll-down, the sub-nav takes its place at the top; when the header returns, the sub-nav tucks beneath it. Below 760px it becomes a horizontally scrolling row with faded edges, which the hero's preset chips already use (remember the `minmax(0, 1fr)` grid gotcha). It contains no solid button, keeping the "one solid mint button per view" rule. The anchors give the future sub-pages their URLs (`/product#deliver` now, `/product/export` later), and the labels avoid internal component names, as best practice requires.

### Defer to future feature sub-pages

Build these later, in this rough order, each only when its facts and assets exist. **/product/agents** (bring your own agent: setup in steps, supported agents, ACP, no AI minutes) follows the pattern Cardboard's /mcp and Descript's /mcp prove out, with copyable prompts like OpusClip's. **/chatgpt and /claude** (the in-chat app, once install links exist). **/product/export** (per-editor guides: opening a KeyFly timeline in Resolve, Final Cut, Premiere, Avid). **/product/director** (deeper AI explainability, model choice, limits). **/product/footage** (silences, filler words, captions, zooms; also the natural long-tail search pages). **/product/brand-kit**. **/templates** (public gallery, once output quality allows). **/teams and /enterprise** (once the team defines the sales path). **/security or /trust** (once the privacy policy and any certifications are real). **/changelog** (only with a committed cadence). Use-case and "vs" pages come last. Under the site's rules, named-competitor pages stay out entirely. If they are ever reconsidered, they need dated, sourced facts and a stated basis of comparison, per the FTC policy, and EU rules, which this research did not cover.

*Limits of this research:* competitor pages were read through text fetchers, so animation, layout and video content were inferred rather than seen. Canva's pages could not be read (HTTP 403). Vendor metrics were not independently verified. No public data compares the conversion of product-page structures, so the plan rests on observed practice and design judgement, not controlled evidence.
